# Further out: things that could make this its own place

These are deliberately less conventional than “add groups” or “add events.” They are experiments to consider after the basic social loop works. None requires turning people into AI personas or replacing the current interface with a dashboard.

## A circle that is allowed to end

A few people want to do something for one evening, one weekend or one month. They get a shared place and a plan, with a clear end. At the end they can choose to repeat it or keep in touch. The app does not assume every good interaction must become a permanent group to maintain.

This could work for a movie discussion, a beginner tennis session, a short making project or a temporary reading group. The agent can help organize the practical pieces and summarize what people explicitly agreed to.

**Small experiment:** a temporary shared conversation around one real activity. Ask whether the limited commitment makes joining easier. Decide separately what happens to the history when the group ends; expiry of new participation does not necessarily mean deleting every person's copy of the conversation.

**Do not turn it into:** forced rotation through strangers, a match queue people must keep servicing, or an automatic “friendship failed” outcome when the room ends.

## Tiny tools for tiny groups

A group might need a simple game, a turn list, a shared prompt, a small poll, a rotating host selector or a scoreboard for a game they are actually playing. The useful object may be much smaller than a new product feature.

A future tool library could start with a few safe typed components. Only after that proves useful should agent-assisted generation of new tools be considered. Human intent and human social content remain the point; code generation is a way to make the tool.

Wayfinder's website/artifact work shows a useful distinction between precise application operations and open-ended creation. Its actual marketing/site builder is not the social product to import. Pangaea's group publishing concepts also suggest that a community can have a small shared surface without becoming a whole separate platform.

**Small experiment:** let a real group choose among a few prepared tools during an existing activity. See whether any earns repeat use.

**Technical boundary:** generated code must not execute with app cookies, private tokens or unrestricted social permissions. A later sandboxed tool can request specific host-mediated actions. Arbitrary generated HTML inside the main authenticated app is not an acceptable shortcut.

## A human trail through a place

Someone makes a short route through places they want to share: a walk, a few artworks, a public garden, a reading spot or a small neighborhood history. Other people can follow it alone, invite a friend or form a plan around it.

The route is about deliberately chosen public places. It does not track the author's movements or reveal their home. A person can write their own notes; the agent can help verify opening times, distinguish a fact from a personal recommendation and maintain source links.

**Small experiment:** one human-curated route with a few public stops and a clear duration/accessibility description where verified. Test it with people who did not help create it.

**Do not turn it into:** a giant stale tourism directory, an automatically generated personality tour or a map that implies precise knowledge of where users are.

## Introductions through people who want to introduce

Someone asks a friend whether they know another person interested in a particular activity. The friend can choose to pass along the request. Each person controls the next disclosure and whether to accept.

The agent can help with wording, keep the request's purpose clear and track what was actually consented to. It should not expose a hidden social graph, publish mutual connections as social proof or imply that a friend has endorsed someone's character.

**Small experiment:** a manual introduction request between known participants, with explicit approval before forwarding identity or contact details.

**Do not turn it into:** automated friend-of-friend prospecting, a public trust score or pressure on someone to explain a private refusal.

## A short show-and-tell, online or nearby

A few people bring something they care about: a thing they made, a photograph they chose, an object, a question or a niche interest. The app helps choose a time and preserve the useful links afterwards. The enjoyment comes from the people, not a generated agenda.

This fits the Twitter/BFF combination especially well. Public posts show personality; a small shared session adds context. Remote participation can be complete in itself.

**Small experiment:** one short opt-in session with a human host and an ordinary video link or public place. Keep cameras and contribution optional. Ask whether participants wanted another one.

**Do not turn it into:** compulsory personal disclosure, a networking pitch session or an AI host pretending to be a member of the community.

## A question that reaches a willing person

A person has a real local question. They can post it, and people who explicitly enjoy answering that kind of question can choose to see it. An agent can retrieve existing relevant answers before asking anyone to repeat themselves.

This could create useful cross-community contact without treating every knowledgeable person as a free help desk. The difference is a person's willingness to receive the request, not merely a model deciding they look like an expert.

**Small experiment:** an opt-in topic view or occasional owner-controlled digest. No automatic mentions or DMs to everyone whose bio contains a related word.

**Do not turn it into:** a ranking of helpfulness, pressure to volunteer labor, or a system that invents expertise from a photo or a vague profile.

## A private social record someone can take away

If people eventually depend on New Drugs, it should be possible to preserve their own chosen notes, plans and contributions in a documented format. A future client could help a person browse that history or use their preferred agent over the data they actually control.

This is a stronger form of portability than merely allowing a different model to call the same API. It also raises real questions about shared conversations, other people's deletions, public/private boundaries and device security.

**Small experiment:** a useful export of owned data and a simple read-only viewer, before considering synchronization or independent hosting.

**Do not turn it into:** a promise of universal revocation after someone has downloaded a copy, or an excuse to move every private record into a publicly replicated protocol.

## Communities that can outlast the founder

A successful social experiment might eventually need shared stewardship. That could mean trusted local hosts, more than one platform operator, a public operating budget, a chosen legal/fiscal structure or independent communities using compatible software.

The first useful step is much smaller: document how the service is run, give appropriate operators recoverable access, define what they may inspect and make a real handover possible. Open-source licensing or federation would be separate decisions; the current repository should not be described as openly licensed merely because it has a CLI.

**Small experiment:** one additional trusted operator handles a bounded support or moderation responsibility using an audited role, without receiving unrestricted access to people's private lives.

**Do not turn it into:** a governance apparatus larger than the community, or a promise of decentralized control that the implementation does not actually provide.

## Keeping experiments light

A promising experiment should have a small real group, a clear owner, an end date, a reasonable budget and a way to remove it. It should use the normal social controls and respect the same authorship and permission boundaries.

The site can stay playful while becoming more dependable. Those goals support each other when the software takes care of practical friction and leaves the human part to the humans.
